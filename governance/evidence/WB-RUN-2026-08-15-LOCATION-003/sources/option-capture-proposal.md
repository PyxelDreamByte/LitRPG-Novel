## Displayed choice retained verbatim

The selectable card asked:

> How should the lake road meet the shore?

Its shared boundary stated:

> All three preserve the half-day working route. None automatically creates a port, shore settlement, safe zone, boats, buildings, or all-season access.

### A — Single shore work point (not selected)

Displayed effect:

> The road reaches one maintained working point at the water’s edge. It becomes the strongest recurring landmark and concentrates loading and upkeep, but trouble there disrupts most routine access and human pressure stays focused on one shore.

Displayed trade-off:

> Commits us to one primary shore terminal. It still creates no dock, boats, buildings, permanent staff, harbour, or all-season access.

### B — Upper-shore staging + seasonal spurs (selected)

Displayed effect:

> The road ends on relatively firm ground set back from the ordinarily shifting wet margin. Several short seasonal spurs lead to whichever nearby shore contacts are usable, so the staging place recurs while the final alien shoreline changes.

Displayed trade-off:

> Best balance of haulage and seasonal ecology. The staging place and every spur can still flood, erode, ice over, attract wildlife, or become unusable.

### C — Dispersed seasonal access (not selected)

Displayed effect:

> The constructed road ends at a general lakeward approach. People continue along informal or lightly reused paths chosen for current conditions, producing the wildest shore experience but the weakest logistical anchor for a laden return.

Displayed trade-off:

> Lowest fixed infrastructure; highest route-finding, carrying, and dispersed exposure. Informal does not mean random, unknown, or culturally unregulated.

Options A and C remain explicitly nonbinding decision evidence. Their
maintained endpoint, concentrated loading and upkeep, primary terminal,
informal paths, route-finding burden, dispersed exposure, cultural regulation,
and other effects are not setting facts.

## Selected claim boundary

The Author selected only the following direct spatial relationship:

1. The established lake road ends at one recurring upper-shore staging place.
2. The staging place is on relatively firm ground set back from the ordinarily
   shifting wet margin.
3. Several short seasonal spurs extend from the staging place toward nearby
   shore contacts.
4. Which nearby shore contact is usable, and therefore which spur can serve as
   the final approach, can vary with seasonal and local conditions.
5. The staging place recurs while the final shore contact can change.

“Upper-shore,” “relatively firm,” “set back,” “ordinarily shifting,”
“several,” “short,” “seasonal,” “nearby,” “usable,” “staging place,” and
“shore contact” are qualitative author-facing relations. “Short” is relative
to the main lake road. “Seasonal” permits condition-dependent variation; it
does not establish a calendar, fixed rotation, or water level as the only
selection cue.
