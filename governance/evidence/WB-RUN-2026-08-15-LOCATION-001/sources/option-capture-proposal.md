## Spatial options retained

### A — UNEQUAL-SHOULDER APRON (selected)

The fortified core occupies the local crown of the established ridge spur. A
managed apron extends unevenly around it: a deliberately maintained
low-vegetation warning belt lies nearest the fortified perimeter; small,
incomplete contour terraces occupy some usable steeper upper slopes; and
broader fields and grazing ground extend farther across the gentler lower
shoulder. Woodland resumes beyond the managed apron and approaches more closely
on steep or difficult sides. A constructed road descends in bends toward the
lake.

### B — TERMINAL-SPUR CROWN (not selected)

The fort would occupy the end of a spur, steep on three sides and connected by
one defended neck, with upper terraces on one flank and a switchback road. This
would strengthen defence while making access brittle and farm space more
limited.

### C — DOUBLE-KNOLL/SADDLE (not selected)

The fortified core would occupy the higher knoll while a lower saddle or second
shoulder held corrals, workyards, and terraces. This would allow layered retreat
and expansion while increasing maintenance and creating a more exposed outer
zone.

Options B and C are retained only as nonbinding decision evidence. Their
defended neck, flank arrangement, road form, corrals, workyards, retreat
sequence, access weakness, maintenance burden, and danger pattern are not
selected setting facts.

## Water disposition

The Author directly added a combined water arrangement rather than choosing
from a displayed water-option set:

1. a spring emerges within the fortified perimeter and is incorporated into
   settlement water access;
2. wells supplement that access; and
3. cisterns provide stored water.

This is one layered access-and-storage arrangement, not proof of three
independent supplies. The spring and wells may draw upon connected groundwater,
and cisterns store collected water rather than create or replenish it. No
alternative water configuration is recorded as rejected in this run.
